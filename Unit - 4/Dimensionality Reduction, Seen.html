The need for dimensionality reduction

Real data often has hundreds or thousands of features: pixels, genes, words. More features sound like more information, but they bring three problems.

We can't see it

Humans plot 2 or 3 axes. To explore 784-pixel digits we need a faithful 2D or 3D summary.

Curse of dimensionality

Space grows exponentially. With 10 bins per axis, covering the space takes 10d cells, so data becomes sparse and distances lose meaning.

Noise, redundancy, cost

Many features are correlated or irrelevant. They slow training, invite overfitting, and hide the few directions that matter.

PCA: the best flat view (geometric intuition)

Principal Component Analysis looks for the direction along which the data is most spread out. Imagine a flashlight casting the cloud's shadow onto a line. Rotate the line until the shadow is widest. That direction is the first principal component (PC1). The most spread means the least information lost, since the gold shadow points stay as far apart as possible and the pink drop lines (the reconstruction error) stay as short as possible.

Notice: the right plot shows captured variance for every angle. Its peak is PC1, and PC2 sits exactly 90° away at the minimum. PC1 and PC2 are always perpendicular, because they are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix.

The recipe

1) Centre the data. 2) Compute the covariance matrix. 3) Find its eigenvectors (directions) and eigenvalues (variance along each). 4) Keep the top k eigenvectors and project onto them. PCA is linear: it can only rotate and flatten, never bend.

Manifold learning: data lives on a surface

The manifold hypothesis says high-dimensional data usually sits near a much lower-dimensional curved surface. A Swiss roll is a 2D sheet rolled up inside 3D space. Locally it is flat; globally it is curved. Points on neighbouring layers are close in 3D but far apart along the sheet.

Manifold methods (Isomap, LLE, t-SNE, UMAP) preserve neighbourhoods along the surface instead of straight-line shadows. Geodesic distance (along the sheet) is what they try to respect, not Euclidean distance through empty space.

t-SNE: neighbourhoods as probabilities (conceptual)

t-SNE turns distances into neighbour probabilities. In high dimensions, each point picks neighbours with a Gaussian whose width is set by perplexity, roughly "how many neighbours to care about". In 2D it uses a heavy-tailed Student-t curve, which lets clusters separate clearly. Then gradient descent moves the 2D points until both probability maps match (minimising KL divergence): similar points attract, dissimilar points repel.

Why a heavy-tailed curve in 2D?

The Gaussian (teal, used in high dimensions) dies off fast; its width follows the perplexity slider. The Student-t (pink, used in 2D) has a long tail, so moderately distant points can sit far apart without a penalty. That is what pulls clusters apart and fixes the "crowding" problem.

Notice: small perplexity gives many tiny fragments; large perplexity merges groups. Cluster sizes and gaps between clusters in a t-SNE plot are not reliable. Trust "who is near whom", not distances.

UMAP: a neighbour graph, then a layout (conceptual)

UMAP starts like t-SNE with neighbours, but builds a weighted graph: each point connects to its k nearest neighbours, with strong links for close points and faint links for far ones. It then finds a low-dimensional layout whose own graph looks the same: linked points pull together, unlinked points push apart.

Autoencoders: learn the compression

An autoencoder is a neural network trained to copy its input to its output through a narrow middle layer. The encoder squeezes the input into a small code (the latent vector); the decoder rebuilds the input from that code. Because the code is tiny, the network must keep only what matters. With linear layers it behaves like PCA; with non-linear activations it can follow curved manifolds.

Use PCA for fast, interpretable, linear compression. Use t-SNE or UMAP to explore and visualise clusters. Use autoencoders when you need a learned, reusable non-linear code.
