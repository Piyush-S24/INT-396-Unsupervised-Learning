1Why reduce dimensions at all

It is not mainly about saving memory. In high dimensions the geometry itself stops working, and every method in this course depends on that geometry.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Almost every unsupervised method — k-Means, DBSCAN, hierarchical clustering, kNN, LOF — has exactly one input: how far apart are these two points? In high dimensions that question stops having a useful answer, because every pair ends up roughly the same distance apart. Reducing dimensions first is what keeps the machinery working.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Just keep all the features — more information is better” is the intuition everybody starts with, and it is wrong here. A feature that carries no signal still adds its noise to every distance you compute. Twenty useless features do not sit quietly in the background; they actively drown the five useful ones. More columns is not more information, it is more noise per comparison.

What goes wrong if you skip it

Your clustering returns groups that are essentially arbitrary, and nothing warns you. k-Means still produces k clusters, DBSCAN still produces a labelling, and the silhouette score still prints a number — they just stop meaning anything. This is the failure mode that looks exactly like success.

Distances stop telling you anything

The core problem
std(D)mean(D) → 0  as d → ∞
Distance concentration. As dimension grows, all pairwise distances converge on the same value. Once that happens the nearest neighbour is barely nearer than the farthest one, so any algorithm whose only input is distance — k-Means, DBSCAN, kNN, hierarchical clustering — has nothing left to work with.

Geometry fails

Distances concentrate, so similarity stops discriminating.

Data gets sparse

To keep the same density you need exponentially more points as d grows — which you never have.

Noise accumulates

Every uninformative feature adds variance to every distance while adding no signal.

You cannot look at it

Nobody can inspect 300 dimensions. Reducing to 2 or 3 is often the only way to see whether structure exists at all.

Two different goals, often confused
Compression wants to keep as much information as possible in fewer dimensions, and wants the mapping to be reversible — PCA and autoencoders do this. Visualisation wants a 2-D picture in which neighbourhoods look right, and does not care about reversibility or about distances between far-apart points — t-SNE and UMAP do this. Using a visualisation method as a preprocessing step, or reading a t-SNE plot as if it preserved distances, is the most common mistake in this area.
Worked example — how fast the space empties as dimensions grow
Suppose you want 10 sample points along each axis, so that every region of the space is reasonably covered. How many points does that need?
  1. In 1 dimension101 = 10 points
  2. In 2 dimensions102 = 100 points
  3. In 5 dimensions105 = 100 000 points
  4. In 10 dimensions1010 = 10 billion points — more than you will ever have
  5. Turn it round: you have 1000 samples. How densely do they cover each axis?points per axis = 10001/d
  6. Evaluate thatd = 3 → 10.0 per axis;  d = 10 → 2.0;  d = 20 → 1.4
At d = 20, a thousand samples give you barely one point per axis — the space is essentially empty, every point is isolated, and “nearest neighbour” has stopped meaning anything. Drag the slider above and watch the distance histogram collapse into a spike as this happens.

2Principal Component Analysis — geometric intuition

Find the direction in which the data is most spread out. Make that the new first axis. Repeat, perpendicular to what you already have.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

PCA is the default first thing to try, and it earns that place by being fast, deterministic and reversible. It runs the same way every time, it tells you exactly how much information each component keeps, and you can map back to the original space — so you can use it as a genuine preprocessing step rather than just a picture.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just drop the features that look useless?” — because information is usually spread across features rather than concentrated in a few, so dropping columns throws away signal that a combination would have kept. PCA builds new axes out of combinations instead of discarding originals. “Why not go straight to t-SNE, it makes nicer pictures?” — because t-SNE gives you no inverse, no variance accounting, and a different answer every run. It is a way of looking, not a way of reducing.

What goes wrong if you skip it

Skip the standardisation step and PCA silently reports whichever feature happens to have the largest units. Measure one column in rupees and another in lakhs, and the first principal component will simply be “rupees” — mathematically correct, completely uninformative, and it will not look like an error.

Covariance and eigenvectors
C = 1n − 1n∑i=1 (xi − x̄)(xi − x̄)T,    Cuk = λkuk
Centre the data, form the covariance matrix, take its eigenvectors. The eigenvector uk is the k-th principal direction and its eigenvalue λk is the variance along it.
Where the eigenvectors come from
variance along u = uTCu,   maximise subject to ‖u‖ = 1
This is why eigenvectors appear at all. Maximising the projected variance over unit directions is the eigenvalue problem — “direction of greatest spread” and “top eigenvector” are the same statement.

How many components should you keep?

Standardise first, unless you mean not to
PCA maximises variance, and variance carries units. Height in millimetres will dominate the same height in metres purely because the numbers are bigger. Unless your features are already on comparable scales, standardise each to mean 0 and variance 1 — otherwise PCA tells you which feature has the largest units, not which carries the most information.
Worked example — PCA on five points, every step by hand
Points (1,1) (2,3) (3,2) (4,5) (5,4). Both features are already on comparable scales, so no standardisation is needed here.
  1. Centre the data on its mean̄x = (3, 3) → (−2,−2) (−1, 0) (0,−1) (1, 2) (2, 1)
  2. Build the covariance matrix, dividing by n − 1 = 4var(x) = 4+1+0+1+44 = 2.5,  var(y) = 2.5,  cov(x,y) = 4+0+0+2+24 = 2.0
  3. Write the characteristic equationdet(C − λI) = (2.5 − λ)2 − 22 = 0
  4. Solve it2.5 − λ = ±2  →  λ1 = 4.5,  λ2 = 0.5
  5. Find the leading eigenvector(2.5 − 4.5)u1 + 2u2 = 0 → u1 = u2 → normalised u = (0.7071, 0.7071), the 45° direction
  6. Explained varianceλ1λ1 + λ2 = 4.55.0 = 90%
  7. Project one point to check(−2, −2) · (0.7071, 0.7071) = −2.83 — its single coordinate on PC1
One dimension carries 90% of the spread. Set the slider above to 45° and the measured variance of the shadows reads 4.5 — exactly λ1, because the top eigenvalue is the variance along the top eigenvector.

3Manifold learning

High-dimensional data is usually not spread through the whole space. It lies on a lower-dimensional surface curved inside it — and PCA, which can only cut with flat planes, cannot unroll a curve.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

PCA can only cut with flat planes. Real data often lies on a surface that is curved — think of a sheet of paper rolled into a tube. PCA will squash the tube flat and land opposite sides of the roll on top of each other. Manifold methods measure distance along the surface instead, so they can unroll it.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Just add more principal components” does not help, because the problem is not how many straight directions you keep — it is that the structure is not straight at all. No number of flat cuts unrolls a curve. “So always use manifold methods” is the opposite error: they are far slower, most give you no way to map a new point in, and they have hyperparameters that change the answer. Use PCA first and reach for these when it visibly fails.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You conclude there is no structure when there is. The two ends of the roll land in the same place, the clusters appear merged, and you report that the data has no groups in it — when in fact it has clean groups that your method folded together.

Why a linear method is not enough

Two points on opposite sides of a fold are close in straight-line distance and far apart along the surface. Manifold methods measure the second one — usually by hopping between near neighbours — which is exactly the distinction PCA cannot make.

The manifold hypothesis
A 100×100 greyscale image lives in 10 000 dimensions, but the set of images that look like handwritten digits occupies a tiny, curved region of that space — parameterised by roughly slant, thickness, size and a few style factors. Dimensionality reduction works at all because real data concentrates on such low-dimensional manifolds, and representation learning is the business of finding the coordinates on them.
Worked example — straight-line distance versus distance along the surface
Take three points on a rolled-up spiral. Measured through space, A and C look close; measured along the curve they are far apart.
  1. The set-upA sits on the inner arm of the roll, C sits on the outer arm directly “above” it, and B sits halfway along the curve between them
  2. Euclidean distance A to Cthe two arms are separated by one thickness of the roll → small, say 0.6 units
  3. Geodesic distance A to C — measured by hopping neighbour to neighbourA → … → B → … → C follows the whole spiral → 9.4 units
  4. What PCA does with thisit projects onto a straight line, so A and C land on top of each other — the roll is squashed flat and the two arms merge
  5. What Isomap does insteadbuilds a neighbour graph, computes shortest paths through it, then lays the points out to match those graph distances → A and C stay 9.4 apart
  6. The consequence for clusteringunder PCA, points from opposite arms appear to be one cluster; under the manifold view they are correctly separated
The ratio here is 9.4 to 0.6, roughly 16×. No number of principal components fixes that, because the problem is not how many straight directions you keep — it is that the structure is not straight.

4t-SNE and UMAP

Both answer one question: can I draw this in two dimensions so that points which were neighbours still look like neighbours? Neither claims anything beyond that.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Sometimes the only question is “is there any structure in here at all?” — and the fastest way to answer it is to look. t-SNE and UMAP exist to produce a 2-D picture in which points that were neighbours still look like neighbours, so your eye can do the detecting. That is a legitimate and genuinely useful job.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not use the t-SNE output as input to k-Means?” is the mistake to avoid. These methods deliberately distort distances and cluster sizes to make the picture readable — so clustering their output means clustering coordinates that were optimised for appearance, not for geometry. t-SNE also cannot place a new point without refitting everything. Reduce with PCA; look with t-SNE.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You report things the plot cannot support. “Cluster A is twice the size of cluster B” and “A and B are far apart, so they are very different” are both unfounded — t-SNE inflates sparse regions, compresses dense ones, and places clusters almost arbitrarily. Run it on pure random noise and you will still get convincing-looking blobs.

Neighbour probabilities in high dimensions
pj|i = exp(−‖xi−xj‖2 / 2σi2) ∑k≠i exp(−‖xi−xk‖2 / 2σi2)
t-SNE, step 1. Turn distances in the original space into probabilities that j is a neighbour of i. Each point gets its own σi, chosen so that it has roughly perplexity many effective neighbours — which is why dense and sparse regions are treated comparably.
Matching them in two dimensions
qij = (1 + ‖yi−yj‖2)−1 ∑k≠l (1 + ‖yk−yl‖2)−1,  minimise KL(P ‖ Q)
Step 2. Place the points in 2-D and define the same kind of probabilities there — but with a heavy-tailed t-distribution, which gives moderately distant points room to spread out instead of being crushed together. Then move the 2-D points until the two probability sets agree.

How to read — and misread — a t-SNE plot

t-SNE

Excellent local structure. Slow on large data. Every run gives a different picture unless you fix the seed. Needs perplexity tuned, typically 5–50.

UMAP

Much faster, scales to millions, and retains somewhat more global structure. Can transform new points without refitting, which t-SNE cannot. Tuned by n_neighbors and min_dist.

Three things a t-SNE plot does not tell you
Cluster sizes are meaningless — t-SNE expands sparse regions and compresses dense ones. Distances between clusters are meaningless — two clusters drawn far apart may be adjacent in the original space. Apparent clusters may be artefacts — run it on pure noise and you will still get blobs. Always confirm the structure with a method that does preserve what you are claiming.
Worked example — computing one t-SNE neighbour probability
A 1-D toy case with four points on a line: A at 0.0, B at 0.5, C at 1.5, D at 4.0. Compute pj|A with σA = 1.
  1. The formula — a Gaussian around A, then normalisepj|A ∝ exp(−‖xA−xj‖22σ2)
  2. Unnormalised weight for B, at distance 0.5exp(−0.252) = exp(−0.125) = 0.8825
  3. For C, at distance 1.5exp(−2.252) = exp(−1.125) = 0.3247
  4. For D, at distance 4.0exp(−16.02) = exp(−8) = 0.0003
  5. Normalise so they sum to 1 (total 1.2075)B 0.731,  C 0.269,  D 0.0003
  6. Read what happenedD is only 2.7× further than C, but its probability is 900× smaller — the Gaussian decays so fast that distant points effectively vanish
This is exactly why t-SNE preserves neighbourhoods and nothing else: anything beyond the local Gaussian contributes essentially zero, so the optimisation has no incentive to place far-apart clusters correctly relative to one another.

5Autoencoder intuition

Train a network to reproduce its own input, but force it through a narrow layer in the middle. To succeed it must learn what is worth keeping.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

PCA is limited to straight combinations of features. An autoencoder learns a curved one, because the non-linear activations let the bottleneck follow a bent surface that PCA would have to flatten. It is the same compression idea, with the straightness restriction removed — and the compression is learned from your data rather than fixed in advance.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“So always use an autoencoder, it is strictly more powerful” is false in practice. PCA needs no training, runs in seconds, gives the same answer every time, and tells you exactly how much variance each component keeps. An autoencoder needs a lot of data, a lot of tuning, gives a different answer per run, and offers no variance accounting at all. On genuinely linear data it buys you nothing for all that cost.

What goes wrong if you skip it

Make the bottleneck too wide and the network simply learns to copy its input across. The reconstruction loss drops to nearly zero, every chart looks excellent, and the model has learned nothing whatsoever — it is an identity function wearing a neural network. A near-perfect reconstruction is a reason to be suspicious, not pleased.

Encoder, bottleneck, decoder
x → fθ → z → gφ → x̂,    loss = ‖x − x̂‖2
The encoder f squeezes the input into a code z with far fewer dimensions; the decoder g tries to rebuild the input from it. The loss is simply how badly it failed. No labels are involved anywhere — the input is its own target.

The bottleneck is the whole point

Autoencoders and PCA are relatives
An autoencoder with linear activations and squared-error loss learns exactly the subspace PCA finds — it is PCA, reached by gradient descent. Everything the autoencoder adds comes from the non-linear activations, which let the bottleneck follow a curved manifold instead of a flat subspace. That is the same distinction as the previous section, now expressed as a network.

How an autoencoder detects anomalies
Train it only on normal data. It becomes good at reconstructing normal data and bad at everything else, so a high reconstruction error flags an anomaly — no labelled anomalies required. The trap is capacity: a bottleneck that is too wide lets the network learn the identity function, after which it reconstructs anomalies perfectly too and detects nothing.
Worked example — what the bottleneck forces the network to learn
An autoencoder on 28×28 greyscale digit images, with a 32-unit code.
  1. Count the input dimensions28 × 28 = 784 numbers per image
  2. The bottleneck keeps only32 numbers
  3. Compression ratio78432 = 24.5× — 95.9% of the dimensions are discarded
  4. So what must the 32 numbers encode?not pixels — there is no room. They must capture slant, stroke thickness, loop size, digit identity and similar factors that actually vary
  5. Check it by reconstructinga trained network rebuilds a recognisable digit from those 32 numbers, so the information really is there
  6. Now widen the bottleneck to 784the network can simply copy input to output: reconstruction error → 0 and nothing is learned
  7. The diagnostica near-perfect reconstruction is a reason to be suspicious, not pleased
The constraint is the teacher. With 32 units the network must decide what is worth keeping; with 784 it never has to decide anything, which is why an autoencoder is only as useful as its bottleneck is tight.
