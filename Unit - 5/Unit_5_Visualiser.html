1Support, confidence, lift and conviction

Four numbers that describe one rule A → B. They are not interchangeable, and reading only the first two is how people end up acting on rules that are worthless.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

A transaction database contains millions of co-occurrences, nearly all of them coincidence. These four numbers are how you separate a pattern worth acting on from two popular items that simply happen to be popular at the same time.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just count the commonest pairs?” Because the commonest pairs are simply the commonest items — bread and milk appear together because both appear in almost every basket, which you already knew. “Why not stop at confidence?” Because confidence is inflated for free by any consequent that is common anyway.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You sort your rules by confidence and act on the top of the list. On the seven baskets below, two rules have identical confidence of 0.600 and opposite meanings — one is a genuine pattern, the other is negatively associated. Confidence cannot tell them apart; lift can.

Support
supp(A) = baskets with Aall baskets
How often the itemset occurs at all. A rule with tiny support may be real but is rarely worth acting on.
Confidence
conf(A→B) = supp(A ∪ B)supp(A)
Of the baskets containing A, what fraction also contain B? This is P(B | A).
Lift
lift(A→B) = conf(A→B)supp(B)
Confidence compared against chance. > 1 real, = 1 independent, < 1 negatively associated.
Conviction
conv(A→B) = 1 − supp(B)1 − conf(A→B)
How often the rule would be wrong by chance, divided by how often it is actually wrong. ∞ means it never fails.
Why some books write supp(A ∩ B) instead
Both are right — they describe different objects. Here A and B are itemsets, so the rule covers baskets holding every item of A and every item of B — that is the itemset A ∪ B, and we count its support. Now write A and B for the events “this basket contains A” — equivalently the sets of transactions T(A) and T(B) — and the very same baskets are the intersection T(A) ∩ T(B), giving P(A ∩ B). Adding items to a set can only shrink the baskets that match, which is exactly why union on itemsets equals intersection on transactions: supp(A ∪ B) = P(A ∩ B). On the seven baskets below both give 3/7 for {Jam} → {Butter}. The one reading that is genuinely wrong is applying ∩ to the itemsets: {Jam} ∩ {Butter} = ∅, whose support is 1.

2The Apriori algorithm

Checking every possible itemset is hopeless — d items give 2d subsets. Apriori makes it tractable with one observation.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

With d items there are 2d possible itemsets — for a supermarket's 10 000 products that is a number with 3000 digits. Apriori makes the search finite by never counting an itemset that cannot be frequent.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not count everything and filter?” Because you cannot even enumerate the candidates, let alone scan the database for each. “Why not sample the transactions?” You lose exactly the rare-but-real rules that are usually the interesting ones — the obvious patterns survive sampling, the surprising ones do not.

What goes wrong if you skip it

Set the support threshold too low and the candidate sets explode until the algorithm will not finish; too high and you are left with only the rules you could have guessed. Neither failure announces itself — one just runs forever and the other returns nothing useful.

The pruning rule
X ⊆ Y  ⇒  supp(X) ≥ supp(Y)   ⇒   X infrequent ⇒ every superset infrequent
The Apriori principle (downward closure). Adding an item to a set can only ever reduce how many baskets contain it. So the moment an itemset falls below the threshold, every set containing it can be discarded without being counted.

Apriori step by step: C1 → L1 → C2 → L2 → C3 → L3, then the rules

The method, as it is done on paper
  1. Scan 1 → C1. Count the support of every single item. The items that reach the minimum support form L1.
  2. Join → Ck. Combine the itemsets of Lk−1 with each other to form the candidate k-itemsets.
  3. Apriori check. Delete every candidate that has a (k−1)-item subset missing from Lk−1: it cannot be frequent, so it is never counted.
  4. Scan k → Lk. Count the remaining candidates in the baskets and keep those that reach the minimum support.
  5. Repeat until a join produces no candidates.
  6. Rules. Split each frequent itemset into X → Y in every possible way, compute confidence = support(X ∪ Y) / support(X), and keep the rules that reach the minimum confidence.
step 0 of 0
pruned, or below the minimum supportfrequent (in Lk)strong rule (confidence ≥ 70%)
Why it is still expensive
Apriori needs one full pass over the database per level, and the join step can still generate a great many candidates. On dense data with a low support threshold that cost becomes the bottleneck — which is the problem FP-Growth was invented to solve.

The same seven baskets, minimum support 3 of 7.

  4. Prune — this is where the saving happens{E,B}, {E,J}, {E,M}, {E,U} and {M,B} all fall below 3 and are dropped

Without pruning, level 3 alone would have had 5 × 4 × 36 = 10 candidates instead of a handful. The largest frequent itemset is {Bread, Jam, Butter} at 3/7 — and that is where the lift-2.33 rule came from.

3FP-Growth

Compress the whole database into a tree once, then mine the tree. Two passes over the data instead of one per level, and no candidate generation at all.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Apriori's cost is one full database pass per level plus a candidate explosion. FP-Growth compresses the entire database into a tree in two passes and then mines the tree — no candidates are ever generated at all.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just use Apriori, it is simpler to explain?” On sparse data with a high threshold it is perfectly adequate. On dense data or a low threshold the candidate generation dominates everything, and FP-Growth wins by a wide margin. “Why not always FP-Growth then?” The tree must fit in memory; Apriori's working set is much smaller.

What goes wrong if you skip it

Insert the items in the wrong order and the compression disappears. Sorting by descending frequency is what makes transactions share prefixes near the root; insert alphabetically and you get a wide, shallow tree with almost no sharing — all the cost, none of the benefit.

FP-Growth step by step: count, order, build the tree, then mine it

The method, as it is done on paper
  1. Scan 1. Count the support of every item and delete the items below the minimum support.
  2. Order list L. Sort the frequent items in descending support; break ties alphabetically.
  3. Ordered items. Rewrite every transaction with its frequent items in L order.
  4. Scan 2: build the FP-tree. Start from a null root and insert each ordered transaction as a path: where a matching child already exists, follow it and add 1 to its count; otherwise create a new node with count 1.
  5. Header table and node-links. Each item in L points to its first node, and the nodes of the same item are chained together.
  6. Mine bottom-up, starting from the least frequent item x: (a) its conditional pattern base is the set of prefix paths leading to the x nodes, each carrying that node's count; (b) its conditional FP-tree is those paths with every item whose total count is below the minimum removed; (c) its frequent patterns are x joined with the frequent combinations in that conditional tree.
step 0 of 0
new nodeexisting node, count + 1item being minedits prefix paths
Why the ordering by frequency matters
Items are sorted by descending frequency before being inserted, so the most common items sit near the root and the maximum number of transactions share a prefix. Insert in a different order and the tree becomes wide and shallow, the compression disappears, and the algorithm loses its advantage entirely.

The saving comes entirely from the frequency ordering: because J and U are commonest they sit nearest the root, so the maximum number of baskets share a prefix. Order the items any other way and the tree stops compressing.

4Anomaly detection, and how it differs from novelty

The same mathematics, two genuinely different situations — and mixing them up is the usual cause of a detector that works in testing and fails in production.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Anomaly detection and novelty detection use the same mathematics on two genuinely different situations, and confusing them is the usual reason a detector works in testing and fails in production. The difference is whether your training data is already contaminated with the thing you are hunting.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just train a classifier on fraud versus not-fraud?” Because you have almost no positive examples — often fewer than 1 in 1000 — and because the frauds that matter most are the ones nobody has seen yet, which by definition are not in your labels. Unsupervised methods can flag a pattern that has never occurred before; a classifier cannot.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You fit a novelty detector on contaminated data. It learns that the anomalies are part of normal, and then obligingly fails to flag them — a detector that is silent for exactly the reason you built it.

Anomaly detection

The training data is contaminated — it already contains the odd cases you are hunting for. You are asking which of these points are unusual. Isolation Forest and LOF (sections 5 and 6) are used this way.

Novelty detection

The training data is known to be clean. You fit what normal looks like, then ask whether a new point belongs. One-class SVM and autoencoder reconstruction error are used this way.

The one-line test
Ask: could the data I learn from already contain the odd cases? If yes, or if you are not sure, it is outlier detection. Only if you are sure the data is clean is it novelty detection.

The problem that makes this hard
Anomalies are rare, so the classes are extremely imbalanced; you usually have no labels; what counts as normal drifts; and the cost of a miss and the cost of a false alarm are wildly different and rarely written down. A detector that flags 1% of a million daily transactions produces ten thousand alerts a day — which no team can review, so in practice it produces none.

A bank's monitoring system raises three flags on one customer's account in a week.

Three flags, three different kinds of anomaly, and only one of them — the point anomaly — would be caught by a simple threshold. Naming the type tells you which method can possibly work.

5Isolation Forest

An odd point is easy to separate from the rest. Isolation Forest cuts the data at random and checks how quickly each point ends up on its own.

Isolation Forest in one minute
What it is

A method for finding anomalies. It builds many random trees; each tree keeps cutting the data at random until every point is on its own.

The key idea

An odd point, far from the rest, is cut off after only a few cuts. A normal point inside a group needs many cuts.

The result

Every point gets a score between 0 and 1. Close to 1: anomaly. About 0.5 or less: normal.

Watch two points get isolated

Split on a random feature at a random value, repeatedly, until a point is alone. Count the splits. An outlier falls out almost immediately; a point in the crowd takes many.

step 0 of 0
How it works, in four steps
  1. Cut at random. Pick a feature at random, then a random cut value between its smallest and largest value.
  2. Keep cutting until every point is on its own. The number of cuts a point needed is its path length.
  3. Build many trees (usually 100), each with different random cuts, and average each point’s path length.
  4. Score. A short average path gives a score close to 1 (anomaly); an ordinary path gives about 0.5 (normal). For reference: s(x) = 2−E[h(x)] / c(n), where E[h(x)] is the average path length and c(n) the average path length expected for n points.
How quickly the point is isolatedScoreMeaning
after very few cutsclose to 1anomaly

Why it is popular
It never measures distances between points; it only counts cuts. That makes it fast, even on millions of rows, so it is often the first method to try.
Practice: Isolation Forest
  1. Answer: state the main idea in one or two lines.Isolation Forest finds anomalies without measuring any distances between points. How does it do it?
    It cuts the data at random again and again. An odd point ends up on its own after only a few cuts; a normal point inside a group needs many cuts. Few cuts means anomaly.
  2. Find: which point is more likely an anomaly? Give the reason.In one isolation tree, point P is on its own after 2 cuts and point Q after 7 cuts.
    P. It needed fewer cuts, so it is easier to separate from the rest.
  3. Answer: what does each score mean?Isolation Forest gives point A a score of 0.92 and point B a score of 0.48.
    A (0.92, close to 1) is an anomaly. B (0.48, about 0.5) is normal.
  4. Answer: give the reason.Isolation Forest builds many trees, usually 100, instead of just one.
    The cuts are random, so one tree can be lucky or unlucky for a point. Averaging many trees gives a steady result.
  5. Answer: true or false? Give the reason.“Isolation Forest is slow on big data because it measures the distance between every pair of points.”
    False. It never measures distances; it only counts cuts. That is why it is fast, even on millions of rows.

6Local Outlier Factor

LOF asks one question for every point: is this point in a much emptier spot than its neighbours are? If yes, it is an outlier.

LOF in one minute
What it is

A method for finding anomalies by comparing how crowded each point’s neighbourhood is with how crowded its neighbours’ neighbourhoods are.

The key idea

A normal point is about as crowded as its neighbours. An outlier sits in an empty spot while its neighbours sit in crowded ones.

The result

LOF about 1: normal. LOF clearly above 1 (for example 1.5 or more): outlier.

See it on a picture

Each circle reaches a point’s 3 nearest neighbours. A small circle means a crowded spot; a big circle means an empty one.

step 0 of 0
the point being checkedits 3 nearest neighboursflagged as an outlier
How it works, in three steps
  1. Find the neighbours. For every point, find its k nearest neighbours (for example k = 3).
  2. Measure how crowded it is. The closer its neighbours, the more crowded the point’s spot. This is its local density.
  3. Compare. LOF = how crowded the neighbours are ÷ how crowded the point is. About 1 means normal; much more than 1 means outlier.
Why “local” matters
Some groups are naturally spread out, others are tight. A point just outside a tight group can be closer to its neighbours than normal points of a spread-out group are to theirs, so one distance rule for the whole data would miss it. LOF compares each point only with its own neighbours, so it catches it.
Isolation Forest or LOF?
Isolation Forest is faster on very large data. LOF is better when the data has groups of different density, because it judges each point locally.
Practice: Local Outlier Factor
  1. Answer: what two things does LOF compare?LOF gives every point a score by comparing two things.
    How crowded the point’s own neighbourhood is, and how crowded its neighbours’ neighbourhoods are.
  2. Find: which point is the outlier? Give the reason.Point A has LOF = 1.02 and point B has LOF = 4.5.
    B. Its spot is about 4.5 times emptier than its neighbours’ spots. A (about 1) is as crowded as its neighbours, so it is normal.
  3. Answer: why is it called “local”?The method is called Local Outlier Factor.
    Each point is compared only with its own neighbours, not with the whole data. So a spread-out group is not flagged just for being spread out, and a point just outside a tight group is still caught.
  4. Answer: which method suits better, LOF or Isolation Forest? Give the reason.A dataset has one tight group and one spread-out group, and you need the points that are odd compared with their own neighbours.
    LOF, because it judges each point against its own neighbours. Isolation Forest only looks at how easy a point is to cut off from all the data.

7Applications: market basket, cybersecurity and fraud

Three settings where these two families of method do the work.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

These methods earn their place wherever labels arrive late, incompletely, or describe only attacks that have already happened. A method that needs no labels can flag the pattern nobody has seen before — which, in fraud and intrusion detection, is the only kind that matters.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not a supervised model trained on confirmed fraud?” Use one — but it can only recognise fraud that resembles past fraud, and attackers change tactics precisely to avoid that. The usual production design is both: unsupervised detection generates candidates, a supervised model ranks them.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You set the threshold by the model's statistics instead of by how many alerts a team can review. A detector flagging 1% of a million daily transactions produces ten thousand alerts; nobody reads them, so within a fortnight the system catches nothing despite excellent AUC.

Market basket analysis

Store layout, bundling, and recommendation. The classic lesson is that the valuable rules are the surprising ones — high lift on items nobody would have paired. A rule linking bread and butter has high confidence and tells you nothing you did not know.

Intrusion detection

Normal network traffic forms dense regions in feature space; an intrusion is a connection pattern outside them. Unsupervised methods matter here because the attacks that hurt most are the ones nobody has a signature for yet.

Payment fraud

Overwhelmingly imbalanced — often fewer than one in a thousand. Anomaly detection generates candidates and a supervised model (trained on confirmed fraud) ranks them, because neither approach is sufficient alone.

Why unsupervised, specifically

In all three, labels arrive late, are incomplete, and describe attacks that have already happened. A method that needs no labels can flag the pattern that has never been seen before — which is the only kind that matters.

Where the thresholds actually bite

The number that decides whether the system is used
Not accuracy, not AUC — how many alerts a day, against how many a team can genuinely investigate. A detector with excellent statistics that produces more alerts than anyone can read is worth nothing, because within a fortnight everybody ignores it. The threshold is set by the review capacity, and the model is then judged on how much real fraud it catches within that budget.
Worked example — setting the threshold from review capacity, not from AUC
A bank processes 1 000 000 transactions a day. About 0.1% are fraudulent, so roughly 1 000 real cases. The investigation team can review 200 alerts a day.
  1. Threshold at the top 5% of anomaly score0.05 × 1 000 000 = 50 000 alerts/day — 250× the team's capacity
  2. Top 1%10 000 alerts/day — still 50× over
  3. Top 0.1%1 000 alerts/day — 5× over; the queue grows by 800 every day
  4. Top 0.02% — the only one that fits200 alerts/day → exactly reviewable
  5. Now ask the question that mattersof the 1 000 real frauds, how many land in those top 200? That is recall at the operating point, and it is the only number worth quoting
  6. Suppose the model puts 150 of them thererecall@200 = 1501000 = 15%, precision = 150200 = 75%
Capacity sets the threshold; the model is then judged on recall at that threshold. A model with better AUC that only reaches 12% recall@200 is the worse system here — which is why the headline metric and the deployed metric are rarely the same.
