DBSCAN: Clustering by Density

k-Means asks "which centre are you closest to?" DBSCAN asks a different question: "are you standing in a crowd?" A cluster is wherever points are packed tightly together; the lonely points in between are called noise, not forced into a group.

The Big Picture

DBSCAN = two numbers, ε and MinPts, which together define what "dense" means. Dense regions become clusters; everything else is noise.

A Story: Spotting Crowds at a Mela

Picture an aerial photo of a fair. Every person is a dot. Where is a crowd? You don't count crowds in advance, and you don't assume they are round. You simply look for places where people are packed together.

DBSCAN does exactly this with two rules of thumb:

  • ε (epsilon) — "arm's reach". How far you look around each person.
  • MinPts — "how many people make a crowd". If at least MinPts people (including you) stand within arm's reach, you are inside a crowd.
A vendor wandering alone between crowds is noise. Someone at the edge of a crowd — near it, but with few neighbours of their own — is a border person.

The Definitions Ester, Kriegel, Sander & Xu, 1996

Nε(p) = { q ∈ D : dist(p, q) ≤ ε }

ε-neighbourhood of p: every point within distance ε of p, including p itself.

core(p) ⇔ |Nε(p)| ≥ MinPts

Core point: its neighbourhood is "full enough". Core points are the interior of a cluster and are the only points allowed to grow a cluster.

border(p) ⇔ ¬core(p) ∧ ∃ core q : p ∈ Nε(q)
noise(p) ⇔ ¬core(p) ∧ ¬border(p)

Three Kinds of Point

Core

≥ MinPts points inside its ε-circle. Drawn filled.

Border

Not core itself, but sits inside a core point's circle. Drawn as a ring.

Noise

Neither core nor within reach of a core. Left unclustered, drawn as ×.

1 · The ε-neighbourhood

ε is the radius of the circle you draw around each point. It decides who counts as a neighbour.

How it works

Hover any point on the canvas to draw its ε-circle and count the neighbours inside. The count includes the point itself, so a point alone has |Nε| = 1.

What if ε were not there?

Why it matters: ε sets the scale at which density is measured. Too small and clusters shatter into noise; too large and separate crowds merge into one. Without a finite ε the word "neighbour" has no meaning at all.

2 · MinPts

MinPts is the density threshold. It says how many neighbours (including the point itself) turn a point into a core point.

How it works

The same points, the same ε. Slide MinPts and watch points change role: filled (core) → ring (border) → × (noise). Only core points can extend a cluster, so raising MinPts makes clusters shrink and outliers fall away.

What if MinPts were not there?

Rule of thumb: MinPts ≥ D + 1 for D-dimensional data (so MinPts ≥ 3 in 2-D); a common default is MinPts = 2·D, i.e. 4 here. Noisier data → larger MinPts.

3 · Noise and Border Points

Two blobs joined by a thin bridge of points, with a few outliers scattered around. This is where the noise label and the border rule earn their keep.

How it works

A border point joins a cluster because a core point can reach it, but it is not allowed to reach further. A noise point is reached by nobody and stays unlabelled. Remove either rule and the picture changes.

4 · From Neighbourhoods to Clusters

Three definitions chain neighbourhoods into clusters, and the algorithm is just a breadth-first walk over them.

Directly density-reachable

q is directly density-reachable from p if q ∈ Nε(p) and p is a core point. It is one-way: a border q cannot reach p back.

Density-reachable

q is density-reachable from p if there is a chain p = p₁ → p₂ → … → q where each step is directly reachable. Every point in the chain except the last must be core.

Density-connected

p and q are density-connected if some core point o reaches both. Two border points on opposite sides of a cluster are not reachable from each other, but they are connected. A cluster = a maximal set of density-connected points.

The algorithm, one point at a time

1

Pick an unvisited point p. Count |Nε(p)|.

2

If p is not core, mark it noise (for now) and go back to step 1.

3

If p is core, start a new cluster and put all of p's neighbours in a queue.

4

Pop a point from the queue. If it was noise, it becomes a border point of this cluster. If unvisited, label it; if it is core too, push its neighbours into the queue.

5

Repeat until the queue is empty — the cluster cannot grow any further. Return to step 1 until every point is visited.

Grey dots are not yet visited. The dashed circle is the ε-neighbourhood of the point being examined right now.

5 · Choosing ε and MinPts

There is no "right" ε hidden in the data, but the data does tell you where the sensible range is — and section 6 shows how to check the pick.

The k-distance plot

For every point, compute the distance to its (MinPts − 1)-th nearest other point — that is, the smallest ε that would make this point core. Sort these values and plot them.

  • The flat part of the curve is points inside dense regions: they become core at a small ε.
  • The steep climb at the right is the outliers: they need a huge ε before they have enough neighbours.
  • Pick ε at the knee. Everything under the line becomes core; everything above becomes border or noise.
MinPts first, then ε. Fix MinPts from the dimensionality and the amount of noise you expect; then read ε off the plot. Changing MinPts redraws the curve, because "the (MinPts−1)-th neighbour" moves.

The scatter on the left is clustered with the ε you chose on the plot. Points above the line on the k-distance plot are the ring and × points here.

6 · Are ε and MinPts right? Verifying the choice

The knee gives a candidate. Before trusting it, check what the clustering looks like, how it scores, and whether it survives a nudge. A good pair passes all three.

Check 1 · Read the output

Wrong parameters leave fingerprints in the counts themselves. Compare against what the data should contain.

  • Noise share. A few percent is normal for real data. > 20–25 % → ε too small or MinPts too high. Exactly 0 % with one cluster → ε too large; nothing was excluded.
  • Cluster count and sizes. Many tiny clusters (smaller than about 2·MinPts) are fragments of a shattered cluster. One cluster holding almost every point is a merge.
  • Core : border. A healthy cluster is mostly core with a thin border rim. More border than core means points only just reach MinPts — the result is balanced on a knife-edge.

Check 2 · Score it

Unit 2's validity indices still apply, with two cautions.

  • Silhouette / Davies–Bouldin on the clustered points only. Drop the noise first; noise is not a cluster. Use the score to compare one (ε, MinPts) against another, not DBSCAN against k-Means: both indices reward round, compact clusters and will under-rate a correct moon or ring.
  • DBCV (Moulavi et al., 2014) is the density-aware alternative: it scores how much denser the inside of each cluster is than the gap to its neighbour, so arbitrary shapes are judged fairly. Range −1 to +1, higher is better.
  • External check when labels exist. On labelled or synthetic data, the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) compares the clustering with the truth: 1 = identical, 0 = no better than chance.

Check 3 · Nudge it (stability)

The most reliable test needs no labels at all. Re-run DBSCAN on a grid of ε and MinPts around your choice.

  • If the number of clusters stays the same across a wide plateau of ε, the structure is real and your ε sits safely inside it.
  • If one tick of the slider changes the answer, you are on a cliff — the clustering is an artefact of the exact value, not of the data. Move to the middle of the nearest plateau.
  • Do the same for MinPts: a knee ε that barely moves as MinPts goes 3 → 6 is a sign the density contrast is genuine.
Order of work: MinPts from dimensionality → ε from the knee → verify with the three checks → if any fails, adjust and repeat.

Where DBSCAN shines

  • No need to guess k; the number of clusters falls out of the density.
  • Finds clusters of any shape, because it follows density rather than distance to a centre.
  • Robust to outliers: they are labelled noise instead of polluting a cluster.
  • Needs only two, interpretable parameters: the k-distance plot guides ε, and the stability map verifies the pair.

What to look out for

  • Varying density: a single ε that works for the dense cluster shatters the sparse one (try the "Varying density" dataset above).
  • High dimensions: distances lose contrast, so "within ε" becomes meaningless; reduce dimensions first.
  • Scale sensitivity: ε is an absolute distance, so standardise features before clustering.
  • Border ties: a border point between two clusters is assigned by visit order — a small, known non-determinism.
  • Bridges: a thin trail of points can chain two clusters together (raise MinPts to cut it).
Two numbers, one question. → ε says how far to look, MinPts says how many make a crowd, and DBSCAN finds every crowd of any shape — leaving the wanderers alone.
