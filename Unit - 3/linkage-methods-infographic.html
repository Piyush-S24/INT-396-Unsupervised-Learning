Linkage Methods in Hierarchical Clustering

Linkage tells us how to measure the distance between two clusters when we decide which ones to merge. The same data can produce different clusters, depending on the method.

The Big Picture

Linkage = how we define the distance between two clusters
= how we decide which groups to merge next.

A Story: Grouping Students in a Hostel

Imagine a hostel with 6 students. We want to group them by how similar their study habits are — sleep time, coffee, study hours. Different linkage rules produce different groupings.

1. Single Linkage Nearest Neighbor

Distance between two clusters = the minimum distance between any pair of points, one from each cluster.

Uses the closest pair.

In our story: two groups merge as soon as at least one pair of students — one from each group — are very similar.

Good for: long, chain-like clusters; irregular shapes.

2. Complete Linkage Farthest Neighbor

Distance between two clusters = the maximum distance between any pair of points, one from each cluster.

Uses the farthest pair.

In our story: two groups merge only once even their most distant students are still reasonably similar.

Good for: compact, well-separated clusters; avoids chaining.

3. Average Linkage UPGMA

Distance between two clusters = the average of all pairwise distances between the two clusters' points.

Uses the average of all pairs.

In our story: groups merge based on overall similarity between every pair of students across both groups.

Good for: balanced, real-world results; less outlier-sensitive.

4. Ward Linkage Minimum Variance

Distance between two clusters = the increase in total within-cluster variance that results from merging them.

Marks each cluster's centroid.

In our story: two groups merge when doing so keeps each group's students as tightly bunched as possible, minimizing the added spread inside the merged group.

Good for: compact, similarly-sized clusters; the common default in practice (assumes Euclidean distance).

Why Move Beyond Single Linkage?

It can create a “chaining” effect — one close pair can link two large, otherwise different clusters.

Looks like a chain, not real groups.

Complete, average, and Ward linkage give more compact, meaningful clusters by weighing overall distance or variance, not just one pair.

We reach for complete, average, or Ward linkage when we want: well-separated, compact clusters · stable, meaningful groups · less sensitivity to outliers and chaining.

Dendrogram: The Tree of Clusters

A dendrogram is a tree diagram that shows how clusters are merged, step by step.

The Story: Building a Family Tree

How to Build a Dendrogram

1
Compute the distance matrix
Pairwise distances between all points (e.g., Euclidean).
2
Choose a linkage method
Single, complete, average, or Ward.
3
Start with each point as its own cluster
Every data point begins separate.
4
Merge the closest pair
Combine the two clusters with the smallest linkage distance.
5
Update distances and repeat
Recompute until one cluster remains.
6
Plot the dendrogram
The y-axis shows the distance at each merge.

Example Dendrogram average linkage

• A and B are most similar (merged at the lowest distance).
• D and E are next (small distance).
• C joins the A–B cluster later.
• F is the last to join — the most different point overall.
What the y-axis means: the distance (dissimilarity) at which clusters were merged. Higher = less similar.

Rules to Remember

1
Lower height = more similar.
Clusters merged at a low distance are more alike.
2
The order of merges matters.
The dendrogram records the exact merge sequence.
3
Cut height sets cluster count.
Cut the dendrogram at a chosen height.
4
Left-to-right order is arbitrary.
It shows merge sequence, not similarity.

What to Look Out For

• Chaining effect, especially with single linkage.
• Unequal cluster sizes — possible with any method.
• Outliers can distort distances, especially with single linkage.
• The right cut-off height depends on your goal and data.
• Don't over-interpret small distance differences near the top.
Same data. Different rules. Different clusters. → Choose the linkage method that fits your data, your goal, and the shape of your clusters.
