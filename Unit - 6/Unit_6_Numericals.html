1Cohesion, separation and the identity

The two quantities every internal measure is built from — and they are two views of one number, not two competing goals.

The decomposition identity
SSE + SSB = SST
SST depends only on the data, so it is fixed whatever clustering you choose. Tightening the clusters must therefore separate them further.
The method — what to actually do
  1. Compute the centroid of each cluster (coordinate-wise mean of its members).
  2. Compute the overall mean μ of all points.
  3. SSE: for each point, squared distance to its own centroid; sum everything.
  4. SSB: for each cluster, (size) × (squared distance from its centroid to μ); sum.
  5. SST: for each point, squared distance to μ; sum.
  6. Check SSE + SSB = SST. If it does not, you have an arithmetic error — this is a free correctness test.
  7. Never compare SSE across different k: it always falls as k rises.
Worked example — all three quantities, and the check
Seven points A(1,1) B(1.5,2) C(2,1.5) D(7,7) E(7.5,8) F(8,7) G(4.5,4.5), clustered as {A, B, C, G} and {D, E, F}.
  1. Centroid of cluster 1μ1 = (1+1.5+2+4.54, 1+2+1.5+4.54) = (2.25, 2.25)
  2. Centroid of cluster 2μ2 = (7+7.5+83, 7+8+73) = (7.5, 7.333)
  3. Cohesion of cluster 1 — squared distances to μ1A 3.125, B 0.625, C 0.625, G 10.125 → SSE1 = 14.500
  4. Cohesion of cluster 2SSE2 = 1.167 — far tighter
  5. Total cohesionSSE = 14.500 + 1.167 = 15.667
  6. Overall meanμ = (4.5, 4.429)
  7. SeparationSSB = 4 · d2(μ1, μ) + 3 · d2(μ2, μ) = 91.548
  8. The checkSSE + SSB = 15.667 + 91.548 = 107.214, and SST computed directly = 107.214 ✓
The identity holds exactly. Note that G alone contributes 10.125 of cluster 1's 14.500 — the first arithmetic hint that G is in the wrong cluster, which the next section confirms.
Practice
  1. A cluster has points (0,0), (2,0), (1,2). Find its centroid and its cohesion.
    Centroid = ((0+2+1)/3, (0+0+2)/3) = (1, 0.667). Squared distances: (0,0) → 1+0.444 = 1.444; (2,0) → 1+0.444 = 1.444; (1,2) → 0+1.778 = 1.778. SSE = 4.667.
  2. Your SSE is 40, SSB is 55, SST is 100. What has gone wrong?
    40 + 55 = 95 ≠ 100, so there is an arithmetic error somewhere. The identity is exact, so the three numbers must reconcile. Most often the overall mean μ was computed wrongly, or a cluster size was mis-weighted in SSB.
  3. Why can you not choose k by minimising SSE?
    SSE always decreases as k rises and reaches 0 at k = n, with every point its own cluster. Minimising it therefore always returns the degenerate answer. SSE is only comparable at a fixed k — which is what k-Means restarts use it for.

2Silhouette

A verdict per point, not just per clustering — which is what makes it actionable.

The silhouette of one point
s(i) = b(i) − a(i)max [a(i), b(i)]
a(i) is the mean distance to its own cluster; b(i) is the mean distance to the nearest other cluster. Range −1 to +1.
The method — what to actually do
  1. For point i, compute the mean distance to every other member of its own cluster → a(i). (Exclude itself.)
  2. For each other cluster, compute the mean distance from i to all its members.
  3. Take the smallest of those → b(i).
  4. s = (b − a) / max(a, b).
  5. Read the sign: positive = correctly placed; near 0 = on the boundary; negative = in the wrong cluster.
  6. Average over all points for the overall score — but always look at the per-point values too.
  7. Guide: >0.70 strong, 0.51–0.70 reasonable, 0.26–0.50 weak, ≤0.25 no real structure.
Worked example — finding the misassigned point
The same seven points. G(4.5, 4.5) has been put in the left cluster {A, B, C, G}, but it sits between the two groups. Compute s(G).
  1. a(G) — mean distance to its own clusterd(G,A) = 4.9497, d(G,B) = 3.9051, d(G,C) = 3.9051 → 12.75993 = 4.2533
  2. b(G) — mean distance to the nearest other clusterd(G,D) = 3.5355, d(G,E) = 4.6098, d(G,F) = 4.3012 → 12.44653 = 4.1488
  3. Apply the formula4.1488 − 4.2533max(4.2533, 4.1488) = −0.10454.2533 = −0.0246
  4. Read the signnegative → G is on average closer to the other cluster than to its own, so it is in the wrong one
  5. A well-placed point for contrasts(F) = 0.8581; every other point scores above 0.73
  6. Overall average0.7364+0.7624+0.7621+0.8424+0.8565+0.8581 − 0.02467 = 0.6848
Overall 0.6848 reads as “reasonable structure” and tells you nothing. The single negative value tells you exactly which point to move — which is why the per-point plot is worth far more than the mean.
Practice
  1. A point has a = 2.0 and b = 6.0. Compute its silhouette.
    (6.0 − 2.0)/max(2.0, 6.0) = 4.0/6.0 = 0.667 — comfortably inside the right cluster.
  2. Another has a = 5.0 and b = 5.0. What is its silhouette, and what does it mean?
    (5.0 − 5.0)/5.0 = 0.000. The point is exactly on the boundary — equally close to its own cluster and the next one. It could go either way, and a small change in the data would move it.
  3. Overall silhouette is 0.72, but one cluster averages 0.35 while the others average 0.85. What should you do?
    Investigate that one cluster. The healthy average hides it. Likely causes: it is really two clusters that should be split, or it is a diffuse group of points that do not belong together at all. The aggregate score would never have revealed this — only the per-cluster breakdown does.

3Davies–Bouldin, Dunn, Rand and ARI

Three more internal indices and the external pair. Same ingredients, different aggregation — and they can disagree.

Two internal indices
DB = 1K ∑i maxj≠i Si + SjMij,   Dunn = min intermax diameter
Si is cluster i's mean distance to its own centroid; Mij is the distance between centroids. DB lower is better; Dunn higher is better.
External validation
RI = a + ball pairs,   ARI = RI − E[RI]max(RI) − E[RI]
Compare pairs of points, not labels — cluster numbering is arbitrary. ARI subtracts the agreement two random partitions would get for nothing.
The method — what to actually do
  1. DB: compute each cluster's mean distance to its own centroid (S) and the centroid separations (M). For k = 2 there is one pairing.
  2. Dunn: find the smallest distance between any two points in different clusters, and the largest distance between any two points in the same cluster. Divide.
  3. Rand: go through all n(n−1)2 pairs. Count a (together in both partitions) and b (apart in both).
  4. RI = (a + b) / (all pairs).
  5. ARI corrects for chance — always report ARI, never the raw Rand index alone.
Worked example — all four on the same clustering
The seven points again. Our clustering {A,B,C,G} and {D,E,F}; the truth is {A,B,C} and {D,E,F,G} — so G is the single error. Scatter S1 = 1.633, S2 = 0.623, centroid separation M12 = 7.308.
  1. Davies–Bouldin (one pairing for k = 2)1.633 + 0.6237.308 = 2.2567.308 = 0.3087
  2. Dunn — closest cross-cluster paird(G, D) = 3.5355
  3. Dunn — widest within-cluster paird(A, G) = 4.9497 — G again
  4. Dunn index3.53554.9497 = 0.7143; below 1 means a cluster is wider than the gap between clusters
  5. Rand index — count the pairs21 pairs total. Together in both: {AB, AC, BC, DE, DF, EF} → a = 6. Apart in both: 9
  6. RI6 + 921 = 1521 = 0.7143
  7. ARI, after removing chance agreement0.4167
One misplaced point out of seven drops ARI to 0.417 while the raw Rand index still reads 0.714 — inflated by the 9 “apart” agreements any partition gets free. All three internal indices are reporting the same defect in different units: G inflates cluster 1's scatter, its diameter, and scores negative itself.
Practice
  1. Two clusters have S = 1.2 and 0.8, with centroid separation 5.0. Compute DB.
    1.2 + 0.85.0 = 2.05.0 = 0.40. Lower is better, so this is a reasonably well-separated pair.
  2. Min inter-cluster distance is 2.0; max cluster diameter is 8.0. Compute Dunn and interpret.
    2.0/8.0 = 0.25. Well below 1, meaning some cluster is four times wider than the gap separating the clusters — poor separation, or one cluster containing an outlier that inflates its diameter.
  3. Two random partitions of 100 points often give a Rand index around 0.5–0.7. What ARI would they give?
    Approximately 0. That is the entire purpose of the adjustment: ARI subtracts the expected agreement, so chance-level partitions score 0 rather than an impressive-looking 0.6. This is why a raw Rand index can never be interpreted on its own.
