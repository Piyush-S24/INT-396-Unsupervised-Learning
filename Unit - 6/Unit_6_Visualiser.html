1Internal and external validation

A clustering algorithm always returns clusters. The hard part — the part this whole unit is about — is deciding whether those clusters mean anything.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

A clustering algorithm always returns clusters, including on data with no structure at all. Validation is the only thing standing between “the algorithm produced five groups” and “there are five groups” — which are completely different claims.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just look at the plot?” Works in two dimensions, and most data is not two-dimensional — and a t-SNE plot of it will show convincing blobs even on noise. “Why not use external validation, it is more trustworthy?” Because it needs labels, and if you had labels you would probably not be clustering. Internal metrics are what you can actually compute.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You trust an internal metric that is secretly measuring its own assumption. Silhouette, Davies–Bouldin and Dunn all reward compact, round, well-separated clusters — so a perfect DBSCAN clustering of two interleaved crescents scores badly. The clustering is right; the metric is measuring the wrong thing.

Internal validation

Uses only the data and the clustering. Asks whether points inside a cluster are close together and clusters are far apart. Silhouette, Davies–Bouldin, Dunn, cohesion and separation. Available always — which is why it is what you normally use.

External validation

Compares the clustering against known labels. Rand index, Adjusted Rand Index, normalised mutual information. More trustworthy, but it needs labels — and if you had labels you probably would not be clustering.

Internal metrics have opinions about shape
Silhouette, Davies–Bouldin and Dunn all reward compact, well-separated, roughly spherical clusters. Run them on two interleaved crescents that DBSCAN has clustered perfectly and they will report a poor score — because the clusters are correct but not round. The metric is measuring its own assumption, not your answer. Always look at the data as well.
Worked example — choosing a validation strategy for three situations
Three clustering problems. The right check follows from what is available, not from which metric is most sophisticated.
  1. Segmenting 2 million customers; no labels exist anywherenothing to compare against → internal (silhouette) for choosing k, plus stability across resamples to confirm it is real
  2. Benchmarking a new algorithm on the Iris dataset, where true species are knownground truth available → external (ARI), which is far more trustworthy
  3. Clustering two interleaved crescent shapes with DBSCANDBSCAN separates them perfectly, but silhouette scores it poorly because the clusters are not round

The metric encodes a shape assumption. Reading a low silhouette as “bad clustering” without asking whether the clusters are supposed to be spherical is the most common misuse of internal validation.

2Cohesion and separation

The two quantities every other internal measure is built from: how tight is a cluster, and how far is it from the others?

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Cohesion and separation are the two raw ingredients every other internal measure is built from. Understanding that they are two views of one quantity — not two competing goals — explains why k-Means minimising SSE is simultaneously maximising the separation between clusters.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just minimise SSE and be done?” Because SSE falls monotonically as k grows and reaches zero when every point is its own cluster — so minimising it alone always tells you to use k = n. It measures tightness, not correctness.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You compare SSE across different values of k and conclude the largest k is best. It always will be. SSE can only be compared at fixed k — between two initialisations, say — which is exactly what k-Means restarts use it for.

Cohesion
SSE =  ∑k  ∑x ∈ Ck ‖x − μk‖2   (cohesion, within-cluster)
How far points sit from their own cluster's centre, squared and summed. Lower is tighter.
Separation
SSB =  ∑k |Ck| ‖μk − μ‖2   (separation, between-cluster)
How far each cluster centre sits from the overall mean, weighted by cluster size. Higher is better separated.
They are two sides of one quantity
SSE + SSB = SST   (a constant, whatever the clustering)
The decomposition identity. Total spread is fixed by the data alone, so cohesion and separation are not two independent goals — improving one necessarily improves the other. This is why minimising SSE and maximising separation are the same objective, and why SSE alone keeps falling as k grows.

Why SSE cannot choose k for you
Adding a cluster can only ever lower SSE, and at k = n it reaches zero with every point its own cluster. So you cannot simply minimise it. The elbow method looks for where the improvement stops being worth it, which is a judgement call — and that is precisely the gap silhouette was designed to fill.
Worked example — cohesion, separation, and the identity linking them
Seven points: A(1,1) B(1.5,2) C(2,1.5) D(7,7) E(7.5,8) F(8,7) G(4.5,4.5), clustered as {A, B, C, G} and {D, E, F}.
  1. Centroid of cluster 1μ1 = (1+1.5+2+4.54, 1+2+1.5+4.54) = (2.25, 2.25)
  2. Cohesion of cluster 1 — squared distances to that centroidA: 3.125, B: 0.625, C: 0.625, G: 10.125  →  SSE1 = 14.500
  3. Cluster 2 is far tighterμ2 = (7.5, 7.333), SSE2 = 1.167
  4. Total cohesionSSE = 14.500 + 1.167 = 15.667
  5. Separation — centroids against the overall mean μ = (4.5, 4.43)SSB = 4·d2(μ1, μ) + 3·d2(μ2, μ) = 91.548
  6. Now check the identitySSE + SSB = 15.667 + 91.548 = 107.214, and SST computed directly = 107.214  ✓
The total is fixed by the data alone. So tightening the clusters must, automatically, separate them further — they are one objective seen from two sides. Note also that G alone contributes 10.1 of cluster 1's 14.5, which is the first hint that G is in the wrong place.

3Silhouette score

For each point separately: am I closer to my own cluster than to the nearest other one? Its real value is that it gives a verdict per point, so it can tell you which points are misassigned.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Cohesion and separation give one number for the whole clustering. Silhouette gives a number per point — so instead of “this clustering scores 0.68” you get “these six points are fine and this one is misassigned”, which is something you can act on.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just use SSE?” SSE measures only tightness, keeps falling as k grows, and cannot be compared across different k. Silhouette is bounded in [−1, 1], accounts for separation as well, and can be compared across k — which is why it is the usual tool for choosing it.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You report only the average. A mean of 0.68 can hide one cluster scoring 0.85 and another scoring 0.2, or individual points sitting at −0.3 in entirely the wrong cluster. The average is the least informative thing silhouette produces.

The two distances
a(i) = mean dist to my own cluster,   b(i) = mean dist to the nearest other cluster
Compute both for every point. a measures cohesion for that one point; b measures separation for it.
The silhouette of one point
s(i) = b(i) − a(i)max [a(i), b(i)]
Ranges from −1 to +1. Near +1: comfortably inside the right cluster. Near 0: on the boundary between two. Negative: closer on average to another cluster than to its own — it is in the wrong one.

Seven points, one of which is in the wrong cluster

Six points form two obvious groups. Point G sits between them and has been assigned to the left cluster. Step through and watch silhouette find it.

4Davies–Bouldin and Dunn

Two more internal indices, built from the same cohesion and separation ingredients but aggregated differently — and they disagree with silhouette often enough to be worth knowing.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Silhouette is not the only internal index, and the others aggregate the same ingredients differently — Davies–Bouldin averages each cluster's worst pairing, Dunn uses only the two extremes. Knowing more than one matters because they react differently to the same defect.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just use silhouette for everything?” It costs O(n2) because it needs every pairwise distance; Davies–Bouldin needs only centroids and is far cheaper on large data. “Why not Dunn, it is the simplest?” It uses only the minimum gap and the maximum diameter, so a single outlier inflates the denominator and destroys the score.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You pick whichever index flatters your result. When the three disagree, that disagreement is information — usually that the clusters are not the compact spheres all three assume — and choosing the kindest number throws that information away.

Davies–Bouldin index
DB = 1KK∑i=1 maxj≠i Si + SjMij
For each cluster find its worst partner — the one whose combined scatter relative to their centroid separation is largest — then average those worst cases. Lower is better, and 0 is perfect.
Dunn index
Dunn = min distance between any two clustersmax diameter of any cluster
The closest pair of clusters divided by the widest cluster. Higher is better. Because it uses only the two extremes it is very sensitive to a single outlier — one stray point inflates the denominator and wrecks the score.

All four indices on the same clustering

They do not always agree, and that is informative
When silhouette, Davies–Bouldin and Dunn disagree, it usually means the clusters are not the compact spheres these indices assume — different aggregation rules react differently to elongated shapes, unequal sizes and outliers. Disagreement is a signal to look at the data, not to pick whichever index flatters your result.
Worked example — three indices on the same clustering
The same seven points and the same two clusters, with G misplaced. Scatter S1 = 1.633 (dragged up by G), S2 = 0.623, centroid separation M12 = 7.308.
  1. Davies–Bouldin — for k = 2 there is only one pairingDB = S1 + S2M12 = 1.633 + 0.6237.308 = 2.2567.308 = 0.3087
  2. Read itlower is better, 0 is perfect — 0.31 is good, but G's contribution to S1 is what keeps it from being better
  3. Dunn — find the closest pair of points in different clustersd(G, D) = 3.536, the smallest inter-cluster distance
  4. Find the largest distance within any one clusterd(A, G) = 4.950 — again G, now inflating the diameter
  5. DivideDunn = 3.5364.950 = 0.7143; higher is better, and anything below 1 means some cluster is wider than the gap between clusters
  6. Silhouette, for comparison0.6848
All three are reporting the same defect in three different units: G inflates cluster 1's scatter (hurting DB), its diameter (hurting Dunn), and scores negative itself (hurting silhouette). Here they agree — on elongated clusters they would not, and that would be the signal to look at the data.

5External validation: Rand index and ARI

When you do have ground-truth labels, compare the partitions — not the cluster names, which are arbitrary.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

When ground-truth labels do exist — on a benchmark, or on a labelled subset — you can ask the much stronger question: does this clustering match reality? Comparing pairs of points rather than labels sidesteps the fact that cluster numbering is arbitrary.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just count how many points got the right label?” Because there is no “right label” — the algorithm's cluster 1 might correspond to the truth's cluster 3, and renumbering is not an error. Pair-counting is invariant to that. “Why not stop at the Rand index?” Because it is inflated by chance agreement.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You report a raw Rand index of 0.71 and call it good. Most pairs of points belong to different clusters under any partition, so they agree for free — two random partitions already score well above zero. Without the chance correction the number is not interpretable.

Rand index
RI = a + ball pairs = n(n−1)2
Look at every pair of points. a counts pairs the two partitions agree belong together; b counts pairs they agree belong apart. Comparing pairs sidesteps the fact that cluster labels are arbitrary.
Adjusted Rand Index
ARI = RI − E[RI]max(RI) − E[RI]
Adjusted for chance. Two random partitions already agree on a lot of pairs, so a raw Rand index of 0.7 may mean nothing. ARI subtracts the expected agreement: 0 is chance level and 1 is a perfect match. Negative is possible and means worse than random.

Why the adjustment matters

step 0 of 0
Always report the adjusted version
On the clustering below, the raw Rand index is 0.714 — which sounds respectable — while the ARI is 0.417. The raw figure is inflated because most pairs of points belong to different clusters under any partition, so they agree for free. The same applies to mutual information, which is why NMI and AMI exist alongside it.
Worked example — Rand index and ARI on the same clustering
Seven points. Our clustering: {A, B, C, G} and {D, E, F}. The truth: {A, B, C} and {D, E, F, G} — so G is the single error.
  1. Count all the pairs7 × 62 = 21 pairs to classify
  2. Count a — pairs both partitions put together{AB, AC, BC} from cluster 1 and {DE, DF, EF} from cluster 2 → 6
  3. Count b — pairs both partitions put apart9 pairs (every A/B/C against D/E/F)
  4. The disagreementsG is grouped with A, B, C by us and with D, E, F by the truth → 6 pairs disagree
  5. Rand indexRI = a + b21 = 6 + 921 = 1521 = 0.7143 — which sounds respectable
  6. Adjusted Rand index — subtract the agreement expected by chanceARI = RI − E[RI]max(RI) − E[RI] = 0.4167
One misplaced point out of seven drops ARI to 0.417 while the raw Rand index still reads 0.714. The raw figure was inflated by the 9 “apart” agreements that any partition gets for nothing. Always report the adjusted version.

6Stability-based evaluation

A different idea entirely: forget geometry and ask whether the same clusters keep appearing when the data is perturbed. Real structure is reproducible; an artefact of one particular sample is not.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Every method so far judges the geometry of one clustering. Stability asks a completely different question: if I perturb the data, do the same groups keep appearing? Real structure is reproducible; an artefact of one particular sample is not.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just trust silhouette to pick k?” Silhouette rewards roundness, so on non-spherical clusters it points at the wrong k. Stability makes no shape assumption at all — it only asks whether the answer survives resampling, which is a genuinely independent line of evidence.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You take the global maximum of the stability curve. k = 2 is the most stable choice on almost any dataset, because cutting something in half is robust whether or not two groups exist. Stability is necessary, not sufficient — a clustering can be perfectly stable and perfectly wrong.

The procedure

1. Resample

Draw many subsamples of the data — typically 80% without replacement, or a bootstrap.

2. Cluster each

Run the same algorithm with the same k on every subsample.

3. Compare

For each pair of runs, measure agreement (ARI) on the points they share.

4. Choose k

The k whose clusterings agree most across resamples is the one the data actually supports.

Stability across k — and what it picks

Stability is necessary, not sufficient
A clustering can be perfectly stable and perfectly wrong. k = 2 is often the most stable choice on almost any dataset simply because splitting in half is robust — which is why you read the stability curve for a local peak at a sensible k, rather than taking the global maximum. Use it together with silhouette and a look at the data, never alone.
Worked example — choosing k from a stability sweep
For each k from 2 to 8: draw 50 subsamples of 80% of the data, cluster each, and measure the mean ARI between every pair of runs on the points they share.
  1. The procedure for one value of k50 runs → 50 × 492 = 1 225 pairwise ARI comparisons → take the mean
  2. Results across kk=2: 0.94  ·  3: 0.91  ·  4: 0.72  ·  5: 0.58  ·  6: 0.49  ·  7: 0.44  ·  8: 0.38
  3. The naive readinghighest stability is at k = 2 → choose 2
  4. Why that is a trapsplitting any dataset in half is robust almost regardless of structure — k = 2 is nearly always the most stable
  5. Bring in the second criterionsilhouette over the same range peaks at k = 3 (0.68), against 0.52 at k = 2
  6. Combine themk = 3 has high stability (0.91) and the best silhouette — two independent criteria agreeing is far stronger evidence than either alone
Choose k = 3. Read the stability curve for a local peak where another criterion also agrees, never for the global maximum — which would hand you k = 2 on essentially any dataset you ever run this on.

7Interpretability, case studies and ethics

You have found five clusters. Nobody will act on “cluster 3” until somebody can say what cluster 3 is — and that step is where unsupervised learning usually succeeds or fails.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

A clustering that nobody can describe will not be used. “Cluster 3” is not a finding; “high-value customers who return frequently” is. Interpretation is the step that turns a partition into something a person can act on — and it is where unsupervised projects most often stall.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just hand over the cluster assignments?” Because whoever receives them has to decide what to do, and cannot without knowing what distinguishes the groups. “Why not report the cluster means for every feature?” That is the right idea but the wrong emphasis — what matters is which features are discriminative, not which are large.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You name a cluster, and the name becomes the reality. A segment invented for convenience starts being treated as a real kind of person, and then gets different prices, different offers or different scrutiny — which is how a modelling convenience turns into a decision about someone's life.

Why unsupervised results are hard to interpret

No ground truth

There is nothing to check the answer against, so “is this right?” has no direct answer — only “is this useful?”

The clusters have no names

The algorithm returns indices. Naming them is human work, and the name you choose shapes every decision made afterwards.

Unstable to small changes

Change k, the random seed, the distance metric or the scaling and the clusters move. Which of those versions is the real one?

Easy to over-read

People see meaning in any partition. Run k-Means on pure noise and you will get clusters, and someone will explain them convincingly.

Making a clustering interpretable

TechniqueWhat it gives you
Cluster profilingmean of each feature per cluster, against the overall mean — the single most useful thing you can do
Representative examplesthe points nearest each centroid: real, concrete cases people can look at
Discriminative featureswhich features separate this cluster from the rest, rather than which are merely large
Low-dimensional plotPCA or UMAP coloured by cluster — to check the clusters are visibly separated and not an artefact
A surrogate treefit a shallow decision tree predicting the cluster label; its rules are a readable description
Ethical considerations in pattern discovery
Proxy discrimination: you may exclude protected attributes and still reconstruct them — postcode, name and purchase history together can identify ethnicity or religion, so a segment can be discriminatory without anyone intending it. Re-identification: small clusters in anonymised data can single people out. Reification: a segment invented for convenience starts being treated as a real kind of person, and then gets different prices or offers. Consent: people who agreed to a service rarely agreed to be profiled by it.
The question to ask before acting on any clustering
Not “is the silhouette good?” but “what happens to someone who ends up in this cluster, and would I be able to justify it to them?” Everything in this unit — validation, stability, interpretation — exists to make that question answerable. The metrics are the means; being able to defend the decision is the end.
Worked example — turning three anonymous clusters into something actionable
A retailer's customers, clustered into three groups. Feature means are given in standard deviations from the overall mean, so 0 means typical.
  1. Cluster 1's profilerecency −0.9, frequency +1.4, basket size +1.1, discount use −0.7, returns −0.3
  2. Name it from the features that are extremebuys often, spends more, rarely waits for a sale → “frequent full-price buyers”
  3. Cluster 2recency +1.3, frequency −0.8, discount use +0.9 → “lapsed discount hunters”
  4. Cluster 3 — and this is the instructive onefour features within ±0.6 of the mean, and returns at +1.9 → “high returners”
  5. Why cluster 3 matters more than its size suggestsit is unremarkable on everything except one feature, so an average over all features would have hidden it entirely
  6. The ethical check before actingdoes “high returners” correlate with disability, or with a postcode that stands in for income? If so, charging them differently is proxy discrimination even though no protected attribute was used
The useful features are the discriminative ones, not the largest. And the last step is not optional: before acting on a segment, ask what happens to someone who lands in it, and could you justify it to them?
